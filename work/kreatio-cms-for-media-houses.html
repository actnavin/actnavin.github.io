A Multi-Tenant Rails CMS for Media Houses

By Navin Kumar · Ruby on Rails Developer · Aug 2016 – Mar 2019

Backend engineering on Kreatio's own Ruby on Rails content management product — built for media house clients to manage and monetise their content.

Short version: Kreatio Software owned a custom-built CMS as its core product, sold to media house clients who needed to manage and monetise their content. This was product engineering, not one-off client work — the same Rails codebase had to serve multiple media house customers well.

The context

Unlike a typical client-services engagement, this was work on Kreatio's own product. The CMS's customers were media houses — organisations whose core need is publishing and monetising content at volume, reliably, without their editorial or business teams having to think about the infrastructure underneath it.

What I worked on

I built and maintained Ruby on Rails applications and RESTful API services for the platform from the ground up, as part of the team responsible for a product serving multiple media house clients rather than a single one-off build. That "own product, multiple customers" shape matters: decisions had to generalise across clients with different content and monetisation needs, not just solve one customer's specific request.

Alongside feature work, I focused on the less visible parts of running a real product: improving test coverage and reducing production defects across the Rails codebase, and supporting the AWS and Azure deployments and performance work that kept the platform running for paying customers.

The product-engineering difference

Building features for your own product is a different discipline than building to a client's one-time spec. Every shortcut becomes technical debt you'll personally maintain; every API design decision has to hold up for the next media house client, not just the one asking for it today. That constraint shaped how I approached the codebase for close to three years.

Why this matters if you're hiring: nearly three years on one Rails product — as opposed to a rotating set of short client engagements — means I've lived with the consequences of my own architecture decisions long enough to know which ones actually hold up.
