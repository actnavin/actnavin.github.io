Building a Warehouse Management System From Scratch

By Navin Kumar · Senior Ruby on Rails Engineer · Sep 2019 – Oct 2020

Sole ownership of a Ruby on Rails warehouse management system, end to end — plus a hand in order management, the content website, and the consultations product.

Short version: MyUpchar needed a warehouse management system and didn't have one. I built it from the ground up in Rails, owned it alone in production, and picked up related work across the order management and content platforms along the way.

The context

MyUpchar.com runs a health-information and online-consultation platform with a medicine and health-product delivery arm. That delivery side needs a real warehouse operation behind it — inventory has to be tracked, stock has to move, and orders have to be fulfilled accurately. When I joined, that system didn't exist yet in a form the business could rely on.

What I built

I designed and built a Ruby on Rails warehouse management system (WMS) from scratch — inventory tracking, stock movement, and the fulfillment workflow connecting incoming orders to what was physically in the warehouse. There was no existing codebase to extend and no prior engineer's decisions to inherit; the data model, the background job design, and the operational workflows were mine to define.

I was the only engineer on the product. Every bug report and every new requirement — from warehouse staff on the ground and from the business side — came to me directly, with no hand-off layer in between. That meant owning the full loop: understanding the operational problem, shipping the fix or feature, and living with the consequences of that decision in production the next day.

Beyond the WMS

Alongside the warehouse system, I worked across the wider product surface: the order management flow that fed into it, the company's content website, and the consultations product connecting users with doctors. Owning a single product end to end while also contributing across adjacent systems is a different kind of work than shipping features against someone else's backlog — it required keeping the whole picture in view, not just the ticket in front of me.

Outcome

  • Shipped a production warehouse management system from a blank slate, with no prior system to fall back on
  • Improved operational efficiency by roughly 40% through backend workflow automation in Rails
  • Reduced API latency and improved service reliability across the distributed systems the WMS talked to

Why this matters if you're hiring: building a system from zero and being the only person accountable for it teaches a different kind of judgment than maintaining an established codebase — you learn to make architecture calls that hold up six months later, because there's no one else to catch the ones that don't.
