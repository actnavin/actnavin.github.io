12 Questions to Ask Before Hiring a Ruby on Rails Freelancer

By Navin Kumar · Freelance Ruby on Rails Developer & Engineering Leader · Updated 5 Aug 2026 · 7 min read

Twelve concrete questions, grouped by what they actually reveal, plus what a strong answer sounds like versus a red flag.

Short answer: ask about specific past decisions, not general knowledge — "walk me through a time you..." beats "do you know...". Below are four questions each on technical depth, process, and business logistics.

Technical depth

1. "Tell me about a performance problem you diagnosed and fixed in a Rails app."

Good answer: a specific story — a slow endpoint, how they found the bottleneck (query logs, bullet, APM tooling), what they changed (eager loading, an index, caching, a background job), and the measured improvement.
Red flag: generic answers like "I just optimize the code" with no specifics.

2. "When do you reach for a background job vs. handling something synchronously?"

Good answer: ties the decision to user-facing latency and reliability — anything slow, unreliable (external API calls), or not required for the immediate response goes to Sidekiq/ActiveJob.
Red flag: "I use background jobs for everything" or "I've never really needed them."

3. "How do you decide between adding a callback, a service object, or a form object?"

Good answer: callbacks for truly model-intrinsic behavior only; service/form objects once logic spans multiple models or has meaningful branching — with an awareness that callback-heavy models get hard to reason about.
Red flag: "I just put everything in the model" with no sense of when that becomes a problem.

4. "Describe your approach to testing — what do you always test, and what do you skip?"

Good answer: request specs or model specs for core business logic, deliberate about what's low-value to test (e.g., simple delegated methods), and comfortable explaining the trade-off.
Red flag: "I don't really write tests, I just test manually" for anything beyond a weekend prototype.

Process & communication

5. "How do you handle it when you discover the scope is bigger than originally estimated?"

Good answer: flags it early with specifics, presents options (cut scope, extend timeline, adjust budget) rather than silently over-running.
Red flag: vague reassurance with no mention of proactive communication.

6. "What does your update cadence look like on a multi-week engagement?"

Good answer: a concrete rhythm — e.g., async daily notes plus a weekly call, PRs opened incrementally rather than one giant merge at the end.
Red flag: "I'll just message you when it's done."

7. "How do you handle disagreement with a client's technical decision?"

Good answer: states their concern clearly with reasoning, offers alternatives, but ultimately respects the client's call on their own product.
Red flag: either total deference with no pushback ever, or insistence on doing it their way regardless.

8. "What's your availability really like — how many other clients are you juggling right now?"

Good answer: a direct, specific answer about current load and hours per week they can commit.
Red flag: evasiveness, or a commitment that seems mathematically impossible given other stated obligations.

Business & logistics

9. "Who owns the code and IP once the engagement ends?"

Good answer: confirms IP assigns to the client upon payment, and that this should be explicit in the contract.
Red flag: uncertainty, or resistance to putting it in writing.

10. "How do you handle access to production systems and credentials?"

Good answer: requests scoped, individual credentials (not shared logins), comfortable with access being revoked at engagement end.
Red flag: insists on shared admin credentials or seems unconcerned about access hygiene.

11. "What happens if I'm not satisfied with a milestone deliverable?"

Good answer: references a revision process tied to the original written scope, not open-ended free work but not a rigid "pay regardless" stance either.
Red flag: no clear answer, or "that won't happen" with no process behind it.

12. "Can you point me to a Rails project you can discuss in detail?"

Good answer: even under NDA, they can describe the shape of the problem, their specific contribution, and technical decisions — sometimes with a public GitHub profile or open-source contributions as backup.
Red flag: can't discuss any past work with any specificity.

How to use this list: don't run all twelve as a rigid script — pick 4–5 that match your actual concerns (usually 2 technical, 2 process, 1 business) and let the conversation breathe. The goal is specific stories, not a quiz.

Once you've screened with these, the next step is a small paid trial task before committing to a larger scope — covered in our full hiring guide. And if a quoted rate feels off relative to the answers you're getting, cross-check it against typical Rails freelancer rates.

Happy to answer any of these twelve myself. Ask away before you decide.
